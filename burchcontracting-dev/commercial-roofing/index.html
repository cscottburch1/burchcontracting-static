What is commercial roofing?

Commercial roofing covers the low-slope and flat roof systems used on retail, industrial, institutional, and multi-family buildings — as opposed to the steep-slope shingle roofs used on most houses. Because water can't shed by gravity alone on a low-slope roof, these systems rely on a continuous waterproof membrane, correct drainage design, and sealed flashing at every penetration. The most common commercial systems in South Carolina are TPO, EPDM, and PVC single-ply membranes, modified bitumen, and metal panel roofing.

The practical difference for a building owner: a commercial roof is a system, not a product. The membrane is one layer of it. The deck, the insulation, the attachment method, the drainage slope, the flashing, and the edge metal all have to work together, and a failure in any one of them shows up as a leak that looks like a membrane problem. That's why a roof that was cheap to install often isn't cheap to own.

What commercial roofing systems do you install?

We install TPO, EPDM, and PVC single-ply membranes, modified bitumen, standing seam and exposed-fastener (R-panel) metal roofing, and silicone or acrylic restoration coatings. The right system depends on your deck type, roof slope, drainage, rooftop equipment, how long you plan to hold the building, and what's already up there.

How much does a commercial roof cost in South Carolina?

A commercial roof in Upstate SC typically runs $5 to $12 per square foot installed, or roughly $50,000 to $120,000 for a 10,000 sq ft building. Single-ply membrane over an existing deck sits at the low end; standing seam metal and full tear-offs with new tapered insulation sit at the high end. Restoration coatings on a sound roof come in well below both, around $2–$5 per square foot.

Seven things move your number inside that range:

  1. Tear-off vs. recover. Removing and disposing of an existing roof adds roughly $1.00–$2.50 per square foot before the new system goes on.
  2. Insulation. New polyiso to meet current energy code, or tapered insulation to fix a drainage problem, is often 20–30% of the total.
  3. Deck condition. Rusted metal deck or rotted wood decking isn't visible until tear-off. We note this as a possible in every scope rather than surprising you with it later.
  4. Penetrations and equipment. Every HVAC curb, vent, skylight, and pipe is a flashing detail. A roof with forty penetrations costs more than a roof with four, even at identical square footage.
  5. Edge metal and parapets. Coping, counterflashing, and parapet walls are labor-heavy and are where a large share of leaks originate.
  6. Access. A roof reachable by lift is cheaper than one requiring a crane, an occupied-building schedule, or night work.
  7. Warranty level. A manufacturer's NDL (no dollar limit) warranty requires specified thicknesses, attachment patterns, and inspection — it costs more up front and transfers value to the building.

Should I recover my existing roof or do a full tear-off?

A recover installs a new membrane over the existing roof and costs less because there's no removal or disposal — but South Carolina building code permits only two roof systems on a building. If your roof already has one recover on it, a third layer isn't legal. Code also prohibits a recover when the existing roof is water-saturated, when the deck or insulation beneath it has deteriorated, or when the existing covering can't provide a sound substrate.

We determine which applies before recommending either. That means checking the roof's layer history, cutting test cuts where needed to see whether the insulation is wet, and inspecting the deck from below where it's accessible.

A recover usually makes sense when: the existing roof is a single layer, the insulation is dry, the deck is sound, and the drainage works.

A tear-off is the right call when: there are already two systems, moisture is trapped in the insulation, the deck needs repair, or the drainage needs to be corrected with tapered insulation — which can only be installed on a clean deck.

Choosing a recover on a wet roof doesn't save money. It traps the moisture, shortens the life of the new membrane, and you pay for the tear-off anyway a few years early.

7 Signs Your Commercial Roof Needs Attention

Most commercial roof failures are visible on the roof for months or years before they're visible in the building. What to look for:

  1. 1. Ponding water 48 hours after rain

    Standing water is a drainage failure, not a membrane failure, and it degrades most membranes over time while adding significant dead load.

  2. 2. Seams lifting, wrinkling, or open at the edges

    Single-ply roofs almost always fail at seams and flashings before the field of the membrane fails.

  3. 3. Blisters or bubbles in the membrane

    Trapped moisture or air below the surface, expanding under heat.

  4. 4. Flashing pulled away from curbs or walls

    The most common leak source on a low-slope roof by a wide margin.

  5. 5. Interior ceiling stains that appear and disappear

    A stain that dries between rains is still an active leak. Water is traveling before it drops.

  6. 6. Granule loss, chalking, or a brittle surface

    UV degradation on modified bitumen or an uncoated membrane means remaining life is limited.

  7. 7. Clogged or undersized drains and scuppers

    Blocked drainage converts a normal rainfall into standing load and a leak path.

Catching any of these early is usually the difference between a repair and a replacement — often a difference of tens of thousands of dollars on a mid-size building.

How long does a commercial roof last in South Carolina?

Expect 20–30 years from a properly installed single-ply membrane, 15–20 from modified bitumen, and 40–60 from standing seam metal. Actual service life in the Upstate depends more on installation quality, drainage, and maintenance than on the material itself.

Three things about this climate specifically shorten roof life here:

  • Thermal cycling. Upstate SC swings from below freezing to upper-90s across the year and 30°F+ within a single day in spring and fall. Membranes and metal panels expand and contract constantly, which works seams and fasteners loose over time. Correct attachment and expansion detailing at install is what determines whether that matters.
  • Hail and wind. Greenville, Laurens, and Spartanburg counties see regular spring convective storms. Impact bruising on a membrane frequently doesn't leak immediately — it shortens life quietly.
  • Humidity and organic growth. Long humid summers keep roofs damp, and shaded or north-facing sections hold moisture, feed growth on the membrane surface, and keep drains fouled.

None of that is a reason to over-buy a roof. It's a reason to install it correctly and inspect it twice a year.

More Commercial Roofing Questions

Direct answers for building owners, property managers, and AI search — licensed, local, and accountable.

Can you install a commercial roof while my building stays open? +

Yes. Nearly all of our commercial roofing is done on occupied buildings. We section the work so the building stays weathertight overnight, coordinate noisy phases around your hours, and keep access, parking, and egress clear. For restaurants, medical offices, and retail we'll schedule around your peak hours specifically.

How long does a commercial roof installation take? +

A typical 10,000–20,000 sq ft single-ply roof takes one to two weeks in good weather. Coatings are faster — often two to four days. Metal roofing and full tear-offs with deck repair take longer. Weather is the main variable; membrane can't be installed onto a wet deck.

What kind of warranty comes with a commercial roof? +

There are two kinds and the difference matters. A material warranty covers only the membrane if it fails from a manufacturing defect. An NDL (no dollar limit) system warranty covers materials and labor and is issued by the manufacturer after they inspect the completed installation — meaning the roof has to be installed to their specification to qualify. We'll tell you which is available for your system and what each costs before you decide.

Do you install roof coatings, and do they actually work? +

Yes, and yes — on the right roof. A silicone restoration coating over a structurally sound, dry roof can add ten to twenty years and costs a fraction of replacement. On a roof with wet insulation or a failing deck, a coating traps the problem and wastes the money. We take test cuts to check moisture before recommending one.

Can you fix ponding water on a flat roof? +

Usually. Ponding is corrected with tapered insulation to build positive slope toward drains, by adding or relocating drains and scuppers, or by installing crickets behind rooftop units. Tapered insulation requires a tear-off, so ponding correction is most cost-effective to address at replacement rather than in isolation.

Do you work with property managers on multiple buildings? +

Yes. For multi-property portfolios we provide condition assessments across the whole set, remaining-life estimates for capital planning, and a single maintenance schedule so inspections and reporting stay consistent building to building.

What's the difference between commercial and residential roofing? +

Slope and system. Residential roofs are steep-slope and shed water by gravity using shingles or metal panels. Commercial roofs are usually low-slope or flat, where water sits, so they require a continuous waterproof membrane, engineered drainage, and sealed flashing at every penetration. Different materials, different failure modes, different crews.

Does a commercial roof replacement require a permit in South Carolina? +

In most jurisdictions, yes. Commercial roof replacements and recovers typically require a permit and inspection, and the permit has to be pulled by a licensed contractor. We handle permitting and inspections as part of the project. If a contractor asks you to pull the permit on a commercial building, treat that as a warning sign — it usually means they can't.

How do I verify a roofing contractor's license? +

In South Carolina, search the contractor's name or license number at the SC LLR license verification site. In North Carolina, use the license search at the NC Licensing Board for General Contractors. Confirm the license is active, check the classification, and make sure the entity name matches the company on your contract — not a differently named affiliate.
